SKODA Kushaq · Commodity price outlook

Where will aluminium prices go, and what does it mean for the cost of a Kushaq?

Aluminium goes into alloy wheels, housings and engine parts. Its world price moves every month. This walk-through shows, in plain steps, how we forecast that price, which clues the model relies on, and where each piece of data comes from.

The use case

One commodity, one supplier, one car

A cast-parts supplier makes aluminium components for the Kushaq. The supplier buys aluminium at a price tied to the world market, so when the world price moves, the price SKODA pays for the part moves too, usually a month or two later.

What the model looks at

Fourteen clues, grouped into five ideas

Each month, the model reads these clues and asks: when the clues looked like this in the past, what did the price do next?

Say thisA buyer does the same thing in their head: "prices have been rising, energy is getting dearer, copper is rising too, so aluminium may follow." The model does it systematically across 25 years.
What the model learned

Which clues are driving today's forecast

The bars show how much each clue contributes to the current three-month forecast. The arrow shows whether it pushes the price up or down.

Method: a regularised linear model (ridge regression), chosen because every clue has a visible weight, so the result can be explained line by line. Contribution = the clue's learned weight × how unusual the clue is today. Each clue's pull on the price is small, which is why the overall forecast is a mild move, not a dramatic one.

Say thisMostly the model is looking at aluminium's own recent behaviour: it rose recently, and after a rise the data shows prices tend to ease back slightly. Other metals and energy add smaller nudges. Nothing here is a black box.

Can we trust it?

We tested the model on 96 months it had not seen

For each of the last eight years, we hid the future, trained on the past only, made a forecast, and compared it with what actually happened. The simple rival is "no change": assume next month's price equals this month's.

Actual priceModel's one-month-ahead forecast
Say thisWe are straight about this: month to month, aluminium is hard to predict. Our model is marginally better than "no change" for the next one to three months, and no better beyond that. So we use it for the range and the drivers, not for a single exact number, and we do not trust the six-month point forecast. That is why the band widens.
What it means for the Kushaq

Illustrative assumptions: 30 kg of aluminium per car, 60,000 cars a year, full pass-through under an index-linked contract. Replace them with actual bill-of-material and contract data. Only the metal cost is modelled, not the supplier's processing cost or margin.

Decisions this supports

Time the contractIf the range points up, lock prices or volumes earlier. If it points down, hold off.
Set the risk budgetBudget at the expected price and keep a reserve for the top of the range.
Watch the driversIf copper, energy or the rupee move sharply, the forecast updates before the supplier's invoice does.
Prepare the supplier talkShow the supplier the same data when negotiating index clauses and lag periods.
Say thisA move of a few percent in aluminium is worth crores a year on a high-volume model. The forecast will not tell us the exact number, but it tells us how much to prepare for.

Plain-English glossary

Forecast
Our best estimate of a future price, based on patterns in the past.
80% range
The band that actual prices stayed inside four times out of five in past tests.
Clue (feature)
A piece of information the model reads, such as "copper rose 5% in three months".
Backtest
Hiding the recent past, forecasting it, then checking against what really happened.
Error (MAPE)
How far off the forecast was on average, as a percentage of the actual price.
"No change" baseline
The simple rival: assume next month's price equals this month's.
